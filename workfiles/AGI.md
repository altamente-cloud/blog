## AGI

AGI stands for Artificial General Intelligence.

“General” means you can give it almost any task, and it can handle it. Unlike traditional AI systems, which are narrow and task-specific, AGI is not limited to what it was explicitly trained to do.

We already see early hints of this with modern language models. They can answer questions they haven’t seen before and often do it surprisingly well.

But how good is “good enough” to call something AGI?

There’s no strict definition. Most people converge on something like this: an AGI should perform at least as well as an average human across most domains.

That definition has a problem. No human is good at everything. So in practice, AGI starts to look less like an “average human” and more like a *superhuman system* - one that works 24/7, helps at home, assists at work, and adapts to new problems constantly.

If such a system becomes better than humans in *all* domains, we usually call it Artificial Superintelligence (ASI). That’s a different discussion—and a much more dangerous one.

## The Problem with AGI

So is current technology enough to build such a system?

As a software engineer, I see a parallel with the duck test (and duck typing in Python):

> *If it walks like a duck and it quacks like a duck, then it must be a duck.*

Now apply that to AI.

If a system talks like a human, reasons like a human, and responds like a human then what is it?

That is what current LLM systems do. They are probabilistic systems that try to maximize the probability of the next word that is coming out of it to match the one a person would produce. Hence LLMs are imitators. 

It’s clearly not human. But if we strip away the physical body and focus only on language and reasoning, modern systems already get surprisingly close.

And that’s the uncomfortable part. 

One might argue that people do the same thing - producing a word by word out of context. 

But our contexts are inherently different. While llm's context is just words that we are feeding to predict an answer, the human "context" is a very different conceptually.

For an LLM, context is just the text it receives to predict the next token. For a human, “context” includes perception, embodiment, emotions, and lived experience. These are not just more inputs — they are a different kind of process entirely. Let's see what I mean next.


Let’s make that more concrete.

## Human "Context"

When we talk about the human brain, we’re really talking about a layered system shaped by evolution. Different parts developed at different times, and we can trace that by comparing brains across species.

Take one of the simplest known nervous systems: the nematode *C. elegans*. It has only 302 neurons. That’s tiny compared to modern LLMs with billions of parameters. Yet even here, we see something fundamentally different.

First, it’s a biochemical system. You can’t reduce it to just numbers flowing through connections. Signal strength depends on chemical state. For example, under stress or threat, the organism releases neuromodulators like serotonin or dopamine, which directly change its behavior. Even this simple system adapts based on internal state, not just input.

In humans, this becomes much more complex.

At a high level, the brain is constantly building a model of the world. When you act, your senses provide data—vision, sound, touch—and your brain integrates it into a working “simulation” of reality.

But the same machinery runs even without sensory input.

When you think, imagine, or remember, you’re still running simulations. The brain uses stored information instead of real-time input, but many of the same regions are involved. Language amplifies this ability. It lets one person transmit an internal model to another, who can then reconstruct it in their own mind.

There’s another key point: simulation and learning are tightly coupled. As you simulate, you strengthen or weaken neural connections. You’re updating the system while using it.

This is why imagined experiences can feel real.

If someone tells you a sad story, you can feel genuine sadness. Your brain maps that story onto your own experiences, runs a simulation, and produces a chemical response. Neuromodulators are released, and the feeling persists for some time. You don’t fully control it.

All of this shapes how humans respond to the world. Behavior is not just output from input—it’s influenced by internal state, history, and ongoing simulation.

So the question remains:

Is the duck test enough to call a machine AGI?

Without a precise definition, the answer is still speculation.

## Conclusion

So where does this leave us?

If we follow the duck test, the answer seems simple. If a system behaves like a human across a wide range of tasks, we call it intelligent. By that standard, current systems are already moving in that direction.

But behavior might not be the whole story.

Humans are not just systems that produce the right outputs. We are shaped by embodiment, internal state, memory, and continuous simulation of the world. Our responses are not only computed—they are *experienced*. Whether that distinction matters is still unclear.

This is the core tension.

If intelligence is defined purely by behavior, then AGI may already be closer than we think. If it requires something deeper—grounding, internal models tied to experience, or even consciousness—then we are still far away.

Right now, we don’t have a clear way to tell the difference.

And that might be the most uncomfortable part of all.

We may build systems that pass every test we design, yet still not know what they actually are.
